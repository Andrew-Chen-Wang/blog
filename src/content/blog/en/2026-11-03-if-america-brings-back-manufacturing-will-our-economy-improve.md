Reshoring manufacturing is a top priority by presidential administrations. Businesses are bashed for exporting and draining America’s knowledge and wages to China. Yet, if we restore our strong industrial base, will it have been economically worth it, and what is the potential economic upside?

### Part 1: The Reshoring

Economically we’d see a boost but not significantly at first. At first, what we would see is a zero-sum game between America and the competition that it stole from primarily China and Europe. The reason is simple: at first, America brings back some manufacturing to produce mostly goods that America needs to build. It’s unlikely a manufacturing spur occurs for a single invention, though that is the only other plausible way to reinvigorate America’s manufacturing. Instead, it’d be the geographic advantage of urban development, clustering, and supply chain becoming dense and complex from a few industries in a small region. The second issue is that manufacturing enables domestic inventors and businesses to now more cheaply innovate. Let’s say they start innovating: they will now be able to create new categories of finished goods to sell and export. Both producing goods that have incumbent markets and new goods that we invent boosts the economy through new jobs, increased productivity or efficiency through restored manufacturing, process knowledge, techniques for selling or talent experience boosting, and downstream consumption from consumers and businesses. However, what I’d like to discuss is the end, particularly what is the max economic benefit of reshoring manufacturing to America, how does it happen, what is the most economically optimal and wasteful route, and the future of global competition and shaping of global development and blue collar jobs.

There’s an oversimplification of manufacturing that for a nation to have a strong industrial base, it needs the right mix of unskilled, low wage labor, high skilled designers and engineers, and somewhat experienced but not formally educated tradesmen, having a large range of wages. America, so the thinking goes, in the global stage, can’t compete on wages and thus lost manufacturing. In reality, the hollowing out of America’s industrial base was in pursuit of profit and enabled China to take America’s manufacturing process over decades. Though China performs corporate espionage (as did America in its founding, in addition to both countries starting off as textile producers to jumpstart their economies with their low wages), their decades of experience has built process knowledge and experience, an educated workforce that surpasses America’s volume of educated graduates, particularly in hard engineering like mechanical, industrial, or electrical engineering (because America stopped needing them as we offshored manufacturing), and world-class manufacturing experience and ecosystem. For America to restore manufacturing prowess, it needs more than wages.

I’m writing this because I recently took a trip to China, and I noticed two things: firstly, the economy is bad according to everyone, unsolicited. Workers are waiting years to receive paychecks from the government (and hence infrastructure work is halting sporadically). Didi, the uber equivalent in China, are staffed by folks in their 50s and laid off knowledge workers. The second thing I noticed was low wage, unskilled labor was manned primarily by the older generation. Construction, janitors, restaurant owners and workers. If a store is more high end, particularly good restaurants, flight attendants, and retail, the store is staffed by aesthetically pleasing associates. For the majority of people who aren’t aesthetically pleasing, the economy is faring poorly, and even the older generation can’t get a break. The welfare system in China is not as large and developed as America and Europe’s, and, due to the one child policy, their timeline for supporting the older generation while burdening the younger was accelerated. The youth, more educated and subsidized by their families, are unemployed, “lying flat” and doing nothing, or turning down stressful career paths in favor of simple living in the farms or as delivery drivers. Very similar to how many women naturally don’t want to be burdened with children, as shown by the correlation of number of children birthed being negative compared to higher educational attainment, the more educated one becomes, the less likely they want to use their time spent on low wage, unskilled jobs. In China, the movement is called “lying flat” where college educated graduates cannot get a job and choose to lie flat and do nothing. In America, though every generation calls the next one lazy or weird, younger generations are the same way. From farming to shrimping to manufacturing, America’s youth does not want to enter the blue collar job space — as does the Chinese youth. These jobs are menial, sometimes dangerous, and back breaking, even if the wages are better. America is more politically motivated, with America’s youth in low end jobs protesting and demanding higher wages in companies with slim margins like retail whereas the Chinese youth goes around censorship or silently protests by lying flat.

The fact of the matter is that China’s manufacturing was boosted by multiple factors outside of wage, though low wages are crucial if you want to compare certain industries and goods produced. China’s manufacturing prowess thrives even today because a lot of manufacturing is automated, requiring minimal human conjunction. What China did during its fast growth of the 1980s-2010s was taken advantage of its money hungry population and did the manufacturing for the U.S. It simply was more competitive price wise to produce in China. That low end created the initial supply chain ecosystem and wealth to start moving up in manufacturing more complex goods. China clustered their industries close together, witnessing the largest human migration in history to China’s new urban centers. This is where those stories of Chinese cities starting off as fishing villages to eventually become dominant hubs. ShenZhen is the classic example, but my dad’s hometown of Shangrao, with a population of 6 million residents and 1.1 million in its urban core, just 15 years ago still looked like farmlands. The desire for dense urbanization enabled their economies to move fast. I call this Gluten Web of Supply Chains or Manufacturing. To make noodles or bread, you need to knead the flour by folding it against itself repeatedly, forcing mixture of different layers of dough. Then, you need to let the gluten become complex or develop by letting it sit. The development of industries and ecosystems requires the same patience as gluten development. That kneading is industrial policy and clustering of individuals closely together to produce competition and a "complex" supply chain. The clustering effect brought accelerated economic effects: talent is ferociously bid for, talent has more access to companies and vice versa, through competition and tons of talent, companies can be born over and over again to innovate (in their case, innovating in manufacturing process rather than finished goods), supply chains are “complex”, selling to multiple industries allowing for parts to be used for several cases at low prices, engineers could take advantage of the plethora of parts to tinker and create new companies, ideas flourish due to people chatting a lot (in a dense city, that means ideas flow faster than a less dense city where people can’t meet each other) and get copied with a lack of international copyright law (the U.S. took 100 years to enact international copyright law, just like China’s play. Starting to see similarities?), ideas and corporate experience from talent travel across several firms during peoples’ career paths, and new wealth growth enables investment in friends’ firms (Silicon Valley workers invest in each other all the time. ShenZhen’s version is tech but GuangZhou’s version is furniture. Interestingly not inventive at first glance right?). The initial low wages kickstarted manufacturing, but the later stages are all about innovation. In the early stages, new companies could produce basic goods like screws. At that time, many robotics and manufacturing tools weren’t in China, so the low wages of China’s populace helped. As all those industries became saturated, existing companies could only compete on efficiency and thus crowded out labor with industrial tools to speed up processes. New companies could only enter unsaturated spaces, primarily producing complex goods that needed specialized tooling and education through college or experience. That cycle is where China is today, becoming the leading manufacturer of complex goods around the world, but also losing out at the bottom. As the manufacturing of basic goods reaches a maxima of efficiency, the only other ways to make the economics of basic goods cheaper for consumers is proximity to the good (ie transportation costs) or labor costs. China’s populace is getting wealthier, the youth no longer want to work low end, dead end jobs, so the basic manufacturing companies are slowly closing and moving to other Asian countries until the basic goods industries reach an equilibrium of local suppliers needing the parts rather than selling globally.

This is step 1 in America’s path to reshoring. Development of basic goods, chronologically from medium complexity to the most basic. My reason is controversial and not concrete. Developing basic goods like screws are already extremely cheap. The only thing America can beat overseas manufacturers in costs in are transit. China has its supply chain ie material costs, comparatively lower wages, and decades of efficiency gains in industrialization knowledge and iteration. Medium complexity goods can still be sold to multiple end buyers whilst developing an ecosystem. These goods would have to be for businesses to take advantage of rather than consumers to enable businesses to flourish financially. This isn’t to argue basic goods shouldn’t start being manufactured in America either.

Manufacturing is already heavily automated, particularly metal parts. They are simply not crafted by people, not even assisted. Yet why aren’t they produced in America? Firstly, some might actually be. America still ranks in the top 2 in manufacturing, depending on who’s calculating, next to China. We still produce several goods like chemicals, most of which are medium or high complexity goods that serve a small number of distinct buyers (not in terms of dollar or number of buyers themselves, rather the number of industries or use cases from purchasers). Secondly, many things are still cheaply made in China but also delivered in high quality. Through decades of competition, China is no longer as the myth goes a cheap, low quality manufacturing powerhouse. They provide all these variables in manufacturing: precision, high quality, and high volume of goods all at once. The key materials in China’s supply chain are also key to proximity costs. It’s not just the finished goods but the input costs such as iron ore extraction which are then made into steel and rare earth metal refining, industries so pollutant that primarily China is the only country so dominant at it. America theoretically could make the raw input costs cheap enough to produce the basic goods like screws, yet it’s unlikely due to environmental laws and NIMBYism as we haven’t innovated in ways to make these industries much cleaner (though strides are being made). To start, America’s best chance is to research how to make the raw goods and basic goods cleaner and cheaper to Americans’ likening and start on the more complex goods end.

Fast-forward, let’s say we’ve researched ways to make raw, basic, and medium complexity goods in America happen through density of people and reshoring of manufacturing through the complex web of supply chains. Where do we see innovation? How much does the economy grow?

The economy simply doesn’t grow that much at first. What we’ve done is enabled America to rebuild its process knowledge, rebuild its complex web of supply chains, and grant people access to cheaper parts, faster innovation through proximity to prototype partners, more ideas through a porous ecosystem via urbanization/networks being closer, and a more experienced labor pool in manufacturing. It theoretically could bring back jobs or we may start finding ways to bring more immigrants to help with manufacturing or building our infrastructure such as more rail. Innovation can happen through inventing new manufacturing methods (or inventing processes and machines China already made, as China is banning exporting complex machinery to Germany to slow down manufacturing growth elsewhere through blocking innovation exportation). This can make goods cheaper via efficiency gains or the off chance it enables inventing new finished goods (like the MRI machine was invented by borrowing a lot of ideas and machines from other industries, or at Apple people are promoted for new manufacturing methods, or at Ford, Tesla engineers are introducing new manufacturing techniques and efficiencies for Ford’s EV debut). With time, America’s inventors and businesses can invent new product categories to sell around the world including China.

AI (ChatGPT/LLMs) is a great example of unlocking a new product category to sell around the world, hugely boosting America’s economy. That was until China started racing in the AI space as well and making it open source. Regardless of digital or physical, anyone sophisticated, but especially China, is world class at copying. With much less capital, China’s LLMs are not only catching up quickly, but, every time a proprietary model is released by OpenAI/Anthropic, the time it takes to catch up halves and is currently only at 2.5 weeks. The only thing slowing them down are the most technologically complex physical goods: semiconductors, chips, and GPUs. It’s been decades since we’ve unlocked a new product category as grand as the internet, and, to me, based on how physics research is going, it’s unlikely we will ever again for a long time. Instead, we can look at a different time: the 1910s-1950s. This marked America’s golden age of manufacturing basic consumer goods beyond textiles, rapidly growing the economy via manufacturing complex finished goods like the vacuum, colored TVs, and the microwave. The invention of tons of semi complex goods in America today could rapidly boost America’s economy. My rationale is that software has come so far compared to when America’s manufacturing hollowing out started in the 1980s that we can digitize more of our goods. Software is the art of making processes more efficient or automated, but it’s particularly good at transmitting information. Smart homes could rapidly improve and become adopted if goods were built cheaper and better by American startups. I liken it to China’s car industry: when I visited, ignoring their EV proliferation and electricity independence and dominance and hence cheapness, the features and amenities in their new cars were insane. Because of the educated software workforce and existing manufacturing base having improved so much, their semi-premium cars were extremely well made compared to BMWs. I rode in BMWs, Mercedez-Benz, and Audis in China as well, pricing each at $60-80k USD, yet I loved the small amenities like coffee machine, fridge, swivel chairs, TV screens, advanced door features, sound system, leather seats of Chinese vehicles a lot more. And for what it’s worth, China’s vehicles retail at the same price as America’s, around $30k USD which is very expensive for Chinese consumers and American. We can see a proliferation of amenity boosts, digitization, and new goods in existing product categories (like smart homes) when manufacturing returns. These are goods that may or may not be found in China already, but I believe the American consumer hasn’t been focused on in a long time in the physical world.

On the other hand, new parts, manufacturing, and talent development can lead to extremely advanced technology proliferating as well. The aforementioned examples of chips, semiconductors, and GPUs are technical examples, but America’s prowess is in capital risk for invention. China’s venture capital, wealth/investor, and loan ecosystem is still much more limited compared to America, and America’s expectation on reputation/branding and high quality has meant American businesses have always needed to be technologically innovative. America’s risk-taking culture is envied by China, the world over, deeply. We’ve generated a system and culture that is so risk-tolerant by law and by culture that entrepreneurship is engrained in our vocabulary like rags-to-riches and bailouts or our desire for politicians to be of modest backgrounds to share identity and empathy. We are also a deeply competitive nation, like China, where we admire those who reach their potential and leaderboards. We admire geniuses and desire to be greats. This means we export tons of industries such as music, pop culture, and more importantly academia. America’s research institutions — though now unwinding in prowess not just from gutting of funding but a lack of peer review, cheating/lying, and funding spread too thin between everyone instead of concentrated — are leaders in academia and furthering knowledge. Though China produces much more PhDs, America’s top research is still more credible than China and has furthered knowledge much more than China, though China is catching up with its population becoming smarter, more competitive, and more meritocratic (China is a connection heavy economy).

I believe it’s possible America finds another avenue of technology that can be developed to an extremely advanced state such as the MRI. America has the capital risk tolerance, the research, and the will and culture to compete and innovate beyond known capabilities. Through the digitization of existing goods or the proliferation of new innovation, America’s economy through innovation can grow: reshoring manufacturing can employ people, redevelop our talent base, possibly include more immigrants to develop new infrastructure or work on unskilled work that Americans don’t want to do (helping our welfare state and older generation), create new goods catered to Americans, and start developing even more advanced, world-class technologies that can’t be replicated.

However, these are marginal improvements in efficiency. They aren’t likely to create new product categories. Even LLMs within the software industry haven’t been new product categories. They’ve mostly been productivity gains for software engineers themselves and minimally new software features.

### Part 2: The Economic Upside’s Maxima

Reshoring manufacturing can enable Americans to innovate, create businesses tailored to Americans, and possibly create new advanced technologies the world could never have imagined achieving. It can employ more people in manufacturing, it can lower prices of goods in America, and more startups can engage the physical world, not just the digital, by being closer to supply chains, prototype designers, and parts. The gluten base of urban density means talent can be developed, experience can be gained, and new inventions in manufacturing and goods can allow a proliferation of new, advanced products.

However, at some point, there is an economic maximum. China’s economy is currently stagnating, leading to a high loss of jobs and businesses. American consumers are tightening household finances meaning more Chinese businesses are losing customers, and China’s economy took a blow from property values falling, meaning Chinese consumers and startups will take less risk and purchase less (China is a saving consumer base compared to American lavishly spending and taking on credit card debt, debt Chinese citizens can’t even take since there’s no such thing as credit cards).

Even if America created all these new products, they’d likely service mostly the more advanced economies that can afford the prices of these new goods due to American wages and prices domestically simply being higher. China would likely copy the new American inventions and sell them to the Chinese consumer base. America and China would compete on goods that both produce. I believe after awhile, there will be a K curve in how much America’s physical good startup ecosystem will reach. There can only be so many efficiency gains and new innovation before businesses and consumers stop wanting so many new things. China’s economy grew and then stagnated because of America’s appetite for the ever cheaper goods. No one else was competing as ferociously in manufacturing as China, so China grew fast with little competition. America’s reshoring would not see an acceleration as fast as China’s. China assumed the role of manufacturing powerhouse; America will never be able to reclaim it. It doesn’t have China’s lax environmental laws, relatively cheaper labor, and talent prowess. America at best will compete in the medium complexity goods and the basic goods will be produced more for domestic industries as other countries like Vietnam and Bangladesh have even outcompeted China in basic goods like textiles, guitars, etc. (If you ever look into each of these basic goods, you’ll see that China never holds 100% market share; China is losing ground to even lower wage nations.).

What America would need to precipitously increase its economy was if it found a new consumer base to sell to. That consumer base wouldn’t need the most advanced goods, but the medium complexity and even basic goods. I’m looking at Africa, Central Asia, the Middle East, and South America. Though they have many goods, their consumer bases cannot afford the most complex goods even today. For America to boost its economy greatly, it would need to boost other economies.

In China’s case, America and the world were the consumers of the cheaper goods. Before, America was the manufacturing powerhouse as WWII had bombed out every country, and the 1900s still saw what I’d considered a dichotomy of developing and developed countries. America was the producer but was getting more expensive. Today, we all live in modern worlds where governments and societies are digitally connected, learn similar knowledge, and have similar desires as a society such as digital modernization, peace, welfare for the common person, and having the basic necessities of survival satisfied. China adopted America’s position as manufacturing powerhouse. Now, all countries are taking America’s early 1800s industrial playbook: textile manufacturing, water management, lack of copyright laws, corporate espionage, and copying products. Our global economy has made us so connected even our cultures are melding, as discouraged by Middle Eastern nations and China are heavily against the liberalization of their nations through the export of progressive stances such as LGBT, more revealing clothing, more liberal social views, etc. from America and Europe. That means everyone today is now competing with China. It’s a zero sum game from the manufacturing sense; if all nations are competing for the same global consumers and innovation is slow, then consumer prices may lower, but businesses lack profit and drive to figure out how to create new innovative products, only efficiency.

What all these nations trying to compete with China industrially from America, Southeast Asia, India, Europe to South America need are innovation, businesses tailoring to domestic consumers, and a better-off consumer base globally.

Increasing consumers’ wallets in extremely poor nations such as in Africa or war-torn nations in Central America enables more products to be delivered. It is in the best interest of these manufacturing nations, especially the richer ones, is to help boost the economy of the countries that are an economic tier below you. Making others wealthier provides more consumers to purchase goods. In America’s case, helping develop countries in South America, with the geographic proximity enabling cheaper goods’ transport, means basic goods can be made there and transported to America while making competition in producing those basic goods even fiercer in the global markets. It threatens China, especially given XiJinPing’s new policy of owning all manufacturing regardless of price and complexity, but also several other up-and-coming economies. It enables America to have even cheaper goods while taking advantage of its advanced and talented economy to stay ahead. On the flip side, America has never been threatened militarily by South America, the only countries that truly pose a war threat unlike China which would have no reason to invade America but rather start a communist revolution internally sometime in the distant future or trade barbs for international trade, alliance, or government turmoil purposes. Other nations in west Africa could start delivering goods to the US east coast which would be much cheaper than transporting from China, and South America could deliver to the West Coast or Great Plains of America. The only way to develop those economies is to stabilize their regions militarily as seen in El Salvador and start their own industrial practices. This would start off with competing in the lower rungs of manufacturing basic goods, and over time, depending on education and industrial policy, could lead to nations becoming super advanced economies.

Overall, it’s good to see more people doing well economically, but this would selfishly enable Americans to consume cheaper and better while enabling American businesses to eventually sell even more globally. Today, I enjoy watching peace settle and economies flourish such as in Syria compared to the democratic West burning like France through mismanagement.

It turns out American imperialism / peacekeeping might be necessary for nations to thrive, but only if exploitation and independence for such nations remain.
