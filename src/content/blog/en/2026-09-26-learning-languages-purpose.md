I can speak three languages: English, Chinese, and Spanish. I'm learning my fourth now, Syrian Arabic. I want to explore my reasons for learning more languages and go beyond those reasons in how they connect back to me, my wants, and my personality.

I want to first start off with how I even became multilingual. Having been born and raised in America as a Chinese American, I can obviously speak English fluently. Though my parents spoke mandarin at home, I couldn't really speak it myself that well, even though I went to a Saturday school for Chinese (which isn't exclusive to Chinese families, but Indian, Polish, Russian, etc. families have their own Saturday schools pending population density). Growing up, I could understand the dialogue, but I would mostly reply in English. When my Gaga moved from China to live with my family, I had no choice but learn to speak Chinese. Gaga stands for grandma on my mother's side in Sichuan, China (the mandarin way is Wai Po (pronounced in English as "why!?-Poe-uuhhHHH")). In fifth grade, my school started teaching Spanish even though our Puerto Rican population is quite small, but growing up it became handy and quite fun anytime I had to interact with a Hispanic. Finally, my initial inspiration for learning Syrian Arabic was from AP Spanish; I found that because of the Moor Conquest in Spain, the languages share very similar words like sugar, so I thought "how hard could it be?" (It's hard).

## Human Connection

My parents (and grandma) are immigrants, so their English is not quite fluent but good enough for communicating at work. In their downtime, they can put their specialized engineering knowledge to work and only use a niche vocabulary to coordinate with their teams. During dinner, that vocabulary limits conversations. To fully enjoy dinnertime conversations, discussions were always in mandarin or, rather, Chinglish (I'll get to this term later). By learning a language, you can allow the person you're speaking with to express themselves in their full range.

As I adulted, I came to appreciate my introductions to these languages. In NYC, I can go to Chinatowns across neighborhoods and speak to locals. It makes them feel comfortable speaking to someone they can connect with in a foreign environment or at least to a stranger who is a tad less strange than a non-Chinese speaker. Similarly, speaking Spanish at Hispanic restaurants (or to the Ecuadorians selling food out of a cooler at construction sites around America) makes them feel more comfortable. You can see in people's the stress of needing to speak and respond in a language that they're not quite fluent in, i.e. English, drop immediately. Sometimes, that makes for beautiful conversations.

When I moved to NYC, I felt like I was abandoning my grandma. She is isolated at home because there are no other Chinese speakers that are her age that are near her to walk and talk with. Even without the language barrier, the elderly in America are the most socially isolated group, and paid services have sprung up to have someone talk with your elderly because the children are busy. Anytime I watch a reel about the elderly struggling whether financially or mentally (FYI boomers may be the wealthiest generation, but most boomers are not actually well off), my heart melts. On the subway home from Chinatown one day, an elderly Hispanic man sat next to me, and I introduced myself by asking, "¿puede hablar español?" He immediately lit up and started conversing. By the time it was my stop, he was asking for my number, whether I wanted to attend his apartment complex's barbeque -- the rundown. I call my grandma at least once a week for an hour, and I thought doing a small sympathetic gesture by chatting with strangers allowed isolated people, on the off chance that a stranger was, to feel seen. I started chatting with elderly on the subway more just to see if I could provide a slight dopamine rush to anyone who may feel socially isolated.

I don't think one has to learn a language to connect with strangers. I chat with my neighborhood Bangladeshi (the carts that serve with yellow/tumeric-infused rice) halal cart owners. The carts are all owner-run with rotated shifts between the owners, so I basically see the same guy. It's like going to your local bodega consistently at the same time and frequently, then, one day, while waiting for food, you start chatting. They've lived in the U.S. for at least two decades, but their English is still a bit limited like my parents, so conversations aren't obviously the most intellectual. But it's there's some kind of enjoyment in being able to connect with people. I suppose that's the point of friends. But in this case, the owners are 30 years older than me. One time, I just asked questions like what is it about Islam that you like (the other owner is atheist, but one of his children strangely isn't)? That owner jokingly wants to invite me to Bangladesh, gift me an apartment room, convert me to Islam, and marry someone in his highly extended family tree.

Comparing when I speak in others' language versus speaking in English, immigrants' guards are lowered and feel more connected when you speak their language. When you speak someone's language, there might be a sense that you also understand their culture. People's identity are somewhat composed by culture, so language is a better gateway into others' worlds. I enjoy feeling that connection and seeing others enjoying the friendly conversations -- hopefully lightening their moods and mine.

## Human Condition

If you've been reading my blogs/short thoughts, I think about politics at a high level a lot. One of my foundations for my political ideology (practical, balancing act of active changes) is human nature. For example, in politics and economics, I think people are innately lazy. So we innovate to expend less energy, but the average worker generally wants to optimize for the minimal work for their career strategy (regardless of ambition, you always want to find the best path to your goals). However, I can't base my entire human nature philosophy from anecdotes or theory. Humans are complex on the individual level, but societal cultures shapes the mindsets and leans culture towards certain directions. Thus, people interested in politics compare their nation's politics with others' nations current and historical political systems, culture, and issues.

In English, we only have one word for both maternal and fraternal grandmothers: grandma/grandmother. In Mandarin, different sides of the family have different words: the fraternal grandmother is NaiNai, and maternal grandmother is WaiPo. Contextually, it's because family has historically been extremely important. There's an Internet joke that, when reading Chinese history books which are partly fictional like the famous *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* ([highly recommend this podcast by John Zhu](https://open.spotify.com/show/4bVesRYKwVLfePsxsELneP?si=5e35f57c1b944596)), the scale and frequency of warfare in China was so immense that a book would allocate three sentences to stating that a small skirmish had occurred and nonchalantly mention 38 million people died, and it lasted 50 years. 

Because of the constant anarchy and political instability in China, family became the ultimate survival unit and safety net. Instead of courts, families handled arbitration. Instead of institutions like Social Security, family became a safety net. The only reason I know about this and got interested in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms is because I asked my parents "why are there more words for family in Chinese than English?"

Today, China has stable institutions like their courts and more institutional safety guards (though still much less compared to America), and it's quite possible mandarin will lose its familial words, too, with new legal systems stabilizing. Old English had more specific words for certain family members like Eam for maternal uncle. I'm going to do a bit of plagiarism from [this essay or its AI overview](https://www.ukessays.com/essays/english-language/the-impact-of-the-norman-conquest-english-language-essay.php): During the Norman Conquest of England in 1066, the French-speaking Normans took over the English courts and aristocracy, intervening in the system that made broad categories like uncle or aunt. French became the language of power, prestige, and law, speakers started replacing the traditional Anglo-Saxon words with french terms. At the same time, Anglo-Saxon society relied on a bilateral kinship network. Property and blood-feud obligations involved both the mother's and father's sides of the family. Obligations differed based on which side of the family someone belonged to. The Norman feudal system introduced primogeniture, meaning all land and power passed strictly from the father to the eldest son. The mother's family line lost its legal and political relevance regarding land and wealth inheritance, so the words were lost to time.

My one question got me thinking deeper about why connections are so strong and helpful in Asia compared to America. Not that nepotism or connections don't help in America, but the fairness and competitiveness offered to individuals in different markets (talent, business, etc.) allowed America to advance as a society quickly. It's why America cherishes the rags to riches, American Dream, and frontier stories, and our motto -- practically, our American identity -- is having a can-do spirit. Meritocracy enables societies to advance fast (America didn't start off that way either; see The Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883 and The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978).

Meritocracy isn't unknown to the Chinese, rather, the necessity for connections are far more important in China because it's culturally vestigial from the times of anarchy. This has cascading effects: recruited talent in business could be paid well, but they aren't actually the best talent; the best talent could be left out, and China would miss out on it. When building my recruiting business and analyzing global differences, I found it strange that there were so few job boards in China; turns out, connections just matter so much that there isn't really such thing. It's partially why LinkedIn shut down in China (they cite censorship and backlash as the reason, but if their user base was actually large, a company like LinkedIn would never have shut down from such profits); the shallow connections on LinkedIn may be suitable for Americans as a way of introduction, but the shallowness isn't sufficient for advancements compared to deeper relationships in China.

A final joke from Chinese literature: we learn our mistakes from history, but history tends to rhyme because we forget those same mistakes. Emperors of China cast Eunuchs or imperial advisors using exams which is why the GaoKao, China's version of the SAT, is so important because it was historically Eunuchs' exams. Every dynasty starts off meritocratic through reform and ensuring exams are fair and achieve its desired outcome of finding the smartest people. Slowly, in every dynasty, Eunuchs would alter the exams so only their connections would have the material and knowledge to complete it. This made Eunuchs less intelligent; combined with increasing corruption, every time, a dynasty would fail.

## Human History

Etymology itself is historically and culturally interesting. For example, the advancement or context of a society can be shown through the number of colors there are in a language or having multiple words for different shades of blue, respectively. Last example is the Moor conquest of Spain intertwined Arabic with Spanish; a classic example of words that sound the exact same are sugar or Azucar/al-sukkar, but that led me to think what were words that were adopted because of newly introduced concepts or systems. Some include Acequia from al-sāqiyah for irrigation canal. During the Moor conquest, the philosophy of irrigation was stampeded by the Islamic Green Revolution, so words were borrowed to describe the new system for irrigating compared to the Roman version. The Islamic Green Revolution and by extension the Islamic Golden Age was one of the reasons I started learning Arabic, too. I wanted to learn from the Islamic Golden Age and the Arab peninsula's history. The other reason I started learning MSA was because of the Quran. Like the Bible, the Quran is full of history; sure, many parts are fictional, but the Bible is culturally, literarily, and philosophically embedded in America. I told my friend I wanted to read the English version of the Quran to get why terrorists keep citing the Quran and just a general interest since the Bible is so culturally important, but he (a Pakistani) said I would only understand the genuine meanings of the Quran if I read it in Arabic and actually understood the culture embedded in the Arabic language. Finally, my last reason for learning Arabic is to just communicate with people in the Middle East. The Middle East today is embroiled in war, and I was so wrong about the situation there politically by my Jewish and Arab colleagues, that I decided it's time to learn what is up.

But why learn a language? I could read the history and reports in English. I'm definitely not converting to Islam ever, and I mean it's not hard discrediting terrorists' citations of the Quran to justify their jihads. Sure, connecting with people could be helpful to better understand people's feelings about Middle East conflicts. But racist Islamophobia actually inspired me to learn Arabic; not because I want to be performative (aside: the Gaza situation is totally fucked, and Israel needs to stop expanding. Horrible from a long term strategic standpoint, horrible for political stability of the region, and horrible morally. Initial retaliation and urban warfare was justified, but war has to stop) but because languages also embed cultural mindsets.

In Chinese, there are way too many four-character idioms called ChengYu, and they come from thousands of years of historical literature. America is extremely young and thus looks outward for historical lessons. But China has gone through thousands of years of mistakes. I feel like Americans are grounded in ideals and ideology too often because they don't seek back in millenniums of history for answers and problems. I thought maybe the reason my politics are so moderate, practical, incremental, and balancing is from my -- and I quote my mom here -- "moderating" personality; maybe it's cultural; maybe it's educational. But I've found many of the ideals that I've learned to embody are randomly found in some Chinese idiom or history. The reason I say "found" is that my Chinese was horrible up until 2015, and I genuinely don't understand most Chinese idioms until explained to me, but, once I started learning and reading more Chinese history, I realized a lot of my parents' teachings were akin to those Chinese texts and their lessons simply passed down to me. It also helps that my father talked about politics every dinner conversation. [This SubwayTake by @amyyleungg](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/mrxbMwD_5z8) sums up why learning a different language can help alter your mindset or provide interesting lessons. Arabic has gone through thousands of years of history like China, so I intend to approach Arabic the same way as Chinese. Additionally, western education just doesn't cover China or the Middle East, yet their civilizations are much older and their histories can educate us on how culture and politics evolve.

## Human Government

The last reason I wanted to learn specifically Syrian Arabic is because Syria in 2024 took down Bashar al-Assad and formed a technocratic government. I want to one day host a podcast to learn how to build cities and countries. I would like to interview current and former government officials what worked, what didn't work, why, and what opportunities did officials take advantage of when trying to build their city, state, or country. Syria has the potential to make a formidable country with the right ingredients, a somewhat stable government with stable institutions (after a year of chaos), and extended olive branches from governments like the U.S., and I want to interview those officials one day. This is honestly the primary reason for learning Arabic. It also just happens to be the primary Levantine languages offered by language apps.

As I said with connecting with strangers and understanding mindset, the ability to let officials freely express their thoughts would enable much better interview back and forth. I'm also intrigued by the Chinese political system. They have so many car companies, yet BYD won out all of them. They have so many man-made, modern tourist attractions that look historical, you have to wonder why the f--- did you build this? There was historically a ton of corruption in China, I mean even to this day (connections and nepotism isn't limited to just the family:) Bribery works too!) all officials are involved in corruption (as I've learned, officials drag new officials into corruption whether the new one wants to or not through blackmail and lies). They also had access to Twitter, and made the most random tweets. But the Chinese government is dominating the global order, and I want to get to the bottom of it.

The motivation for this podcast is just a life goal of creating a peaceful world again, lift up all lives around the world (though initially my goal was just to revitalize my home state of Michigan), and the only way to do that is through good governance as humans live in organized civilizations. First and foremost is helping my own country, but I intend to maybe one day form a consultancy to see if I can help governments make life better for people.

## Human Identity

People identify with other groups in how they desire to be associated by others, what they get out of that association, and to find commonality. Some associate through LGBTQ+ communities. Some through political identity. Some through religion, their home nation, their home state, or the color of their skin. And people identify with multiple groups (see [why we bucket ourselves into groups in the first place](https://andrewcwang.com/short-thoughts/2026-09-19-stereotypes/)).

Personally, I only recently started identifying as Chinese American because I previously never introspected my Chinese American identity. I never desired to be associated as Chinese American; I just thought of myself as American and Michigander. I was born and raised in Michigan. I love my country, its history, its struggles, and its people who make America what it is. I love my home state of Michigan, the people, its culture. I apparently have a Michigan accent and vocabulary (ope) and, like many states, am fiercely proud of my home state, culture, and political moderateness. I sought out a girlfriend who was Michigander-like (and found one from Canada, close enough; lots of my Michigan friends also want a Michigan-esque girlfriend). My parents work in dominant manufacturing industries (i.e. auto and defense), so I bond with and understand the people from those industries, too.

My Chinese immigrant parents, their motivation for immigrating, their stories, their lessons, and their mindsets are Chinese. Yet, besides immigration, they were uniquely American and fast adopters of it. They planned on living in a "Bread Van" i.e. the classic white van with their toddler child. China was encouraging engineers to learn abroad, so my dad took advantage of the opportunities the Chinese government was dishing out to move the family, but my parents' ideals aligned a lot more with the values of America than with China, so they stayed in America. I had a somewhat Chinese upbringing but even more so American environment. For example, I had an extremely lenient childhood with minimal guidance, a lot of freedom, and a ton of self motivation derived from exploration, patriotism, and potentially genetics. (My dad loves politics and wished his GaoKao score was lower so he'd be a doctor, not an engineer.)

What makes me Chinese American instead of just saying I'm both American and Chinese is the unique culture of being Chinese in America. Except, I didn't have a cliché Chinese American childhood:

I was never bullied for my school lunch, never bullied for being smart, and never got any negative treatment from peers in my life associated with my background. Rather, I have tanned skin which is looked down on in China but looked up in America. I played soccer instead of many individual sports that Asians typically compete in like tennis or swim. In school, my only real connection with other Chinese kids was our shared ethnicity, and being in advanced classes meant the classes were mostly composed of Asians. With other Chinese kids, I may have shared similar childhood jokes about Chinese parents, but that was the furthest extent, and my parents were fairly lenient and provided me and my siblings immense freedom and self responsibility unlike many Asian families. My best friends throughout my life were primarily white, black, and Indian from my AP classes (though, for some reason, I was put in the all white AP government class instead of the all Asian class; sus administration to me), so I just never associated myself with being Chinese American. It wasn't until I entered politics, connected with other Chinese Americans, and saw their struggles, how they're treated, simply for how they looked and identified with. A strange example of treatment: a lot of Chinese American women look at most Chinese American men and think they're a software engineer, find them introverted, and unattractive and kind of look down on them for various reasons (unless they're 6 feet tall, then they're in finance). Again, [stereotype article citation](https://andrewcwang.com/short-thoughts/2026-09-19-stereotypes/); there's no escape from poor treatment by any group; stereotypes are forever embedded in humans.

A friend of mine, Muhan, was presidential candidate Andrew Yang's first employee. He told me after the end of the campaign and having talked with Andrew a lot that every generation of Chinese Americans had it easier than the last through the breakthroughs and slow acceptance of Asian Americans in American society. In retrospect of my socially neutral childhood, I feel immense appreciation for them and sympathy for the historical shit they had to deal with.

After he mentioned that, I reflected on my childhood a bit on what being Chinese American meant so far for me. When I speak with my parents, I speak Chinglish: each sentence uses basic Chinese vocabulary woven with advanced English words because I don't know the equivalent Chinese words. Sometimes, I use Chinese instead of English because there's a phrase that makes more sense in Chinese or the concept doesn't exist in English. For example, some food can smell xiang or fresh, or a seafood dish is xuan or a mix of rich, umami, and savory. Even my parents over time have forgotten Chinese words or find English words easier to use, so they also speak in Chinglish. I concluded that my usage of mandarin offered me a way to learn about being Chinese American because those aforementioned Chinese words had to do with my experiences thus far in my household: food, politics, and a lot about communist China in the 60s and 70s.

So I started learning more about my Chinese side out of keen interest of exploration. I didn't have any desire to be more Chinese American, just extending my knowledge about the Chinese side that I already knew. The ChengYu/idioms, the history, the culture. Those odd late years of learning Chinese through dinner conversations provided me the confidence to explore my interests on the Chinese side: Chinese culture and history and connecting with people by navigating Chinatown and talking with strangers. Recently, I've been cooking childhood food (see [recipes](https://andrewcwang.com/recipes/)) which required asking questions at stores and listening to Chinese chefs on YouTube describe their recipes in 360p (because NYC is primarily canto based food which I didn't grow up eating). I started feeling proud being able to help friends and strangers in Chinese grocery stores, making potluck Chinese meals, and all around going to a Chinese community and feeling like I could blend in. As I was learning more about my Chinese side, I was connecting with Chinese Americans better.

My tipping point of properly associating myself as Chinese American was seeing others' struggles. It's strange to say, but I didn't identify as being Chinese American until I was 21 years old. I simply didn't know much about my Chinese side, but I could recover by finding the experiences we all would share simultaneously as adults. That's when I started connecting with more Chinese Americans who were years older than me, like Muhan, and saw how they navigated their adult life. I personally never dealt with the same struggles socially (and yes, I went to public school), but I hated seeing others' poor treatment but also selfishly thought my luck would not last. In my adulthood, through connections with strangers by how we look, how we get treated, and them sharing their stories, more of my friend groups have become predominantly Chinese American. Though I don't share many childhood experiences with many Chinese Americans, I've found that the groups I've become friends with trust me to be able to connect with them and deal with life together. My anticipation for future potential experiences will only strengthen my identity and connection with my heritage today.
