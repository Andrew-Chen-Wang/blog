I believe businesses are incentivized by profit, and some like VC backed SaaS are always destined for growth that enshittified the service for more customers: they’re either stretched too thin from too large of an audience or they left behind a group like moving upmarket or like Notion ignoring the consumer side or making them feel benefit only from the benefit to firms first as B2B SaaS.

I’ve covered my disdain for growth at all costs in my mom and pop business blog. To recap, the issue is through the power of large cash stockpiles, if used to undercut and/or shutter competitors across the industry or simply buy out tons of companies to control the market through cash, are you able to maximally deliver the best service at the lowest price? I believe that companies serve best when they can maximally deliver to their core target audience. This is wide ranging: mom and pop restaurants are simply mom and pop, and tons of services like handyman or assisted living homes are run by just a few people. Living within the profit range allows consumers to be addressed directly, and, because the intention is not to grow to larger and larger business, the cost stays lower as the employee cost (i.e. owners being employees) and profit margin can be lower.

This is not a bash at all billionaires and trillion dollar businesses. In fact, businesses like NVIDIA, Google, and Facebook simply have a humongous audience of honestly a single nature such that either through being frontier technological development and/or network effects (which apply to all 3 in terms of CUDA, data, and community, respectively) they are doing the best within their niche, and their niche happens to be massive. Software is uniquely adept at network effects, which is why NVIDIA, though the leader in AI hardware, doesn’t have a monopoly as the TPU, Apple Silicon, AWS chips, and China’s rival GPUs compete for shares and model cost.

However, this is to address the issue of all businesses now vying for immense growth. The requirement for growth goes beyond the necessity for profit in order to hedge risk of shaky seasons, catastrophic events, or innovation. Vying for constant growth means adding even more profit margin on top of all of that, and, within software, this is enshittification. The inability for software firms to compete with venture backed firms in a vertical to compete for a specific market segment baffled me for a long time until I realized venture backed firms simply outmanned in terms of sales and engineers bootstrapped firms. Even then, pre-AI, developing SaaS firms was expensive upfront to get both customers and even a product launch. The tide may change now with AI removing high engineering costs to launch, so we may see more bootstrapped firms in verticals that were traditionally for venture backed firms only, and in verticals that weren’t large enough for bootstrapped firms to want to gun for. HR, ATS, and CRMs have huge SMB market segments allowing for market entrants to be bootstrapped. Now, I believe smaller, niche market segments may become available for bootstrap firms to compete in like FinTech, compliance, ISA, etc. However, growth in itself is not evil; going upmarket is natural if the opportunity arises and isn't filled by competitors satisfactorily. However, the entire software industry’s motto of growth at all cost are incentivized by venture capital which leads to bootstrapped firms getting outcompeted by venture backed firms which led to the enshittification effects by downstream customers.

Taking a step back, the way I view money is extremely neutral. I view money as a means for a human to advance one’s own initiatives. The act of saving money is for hedging the future for literal survival via shelter and food. Historically, that’s what money has always been, not some root of all evil. Evil is conducted by people who choose to wield money in the form of power which comes in many forms outside of money, too. Focusing on just cash though, there are distortionary market effects that cash can do. For instance, heavily capitalized companies can undercut competitors so much to shut out the entire industry in a region making a forced monopoly:

- This is what PE firms are doing with acquiring en masse handyman services
- PE firms also acquire tons of real estate to forever extract rent i.e. Blackstone
- Restaurant groups and grocery stores. An example, Luckin Coffee or Starbucks use their multinational profits to afford the rents of Manhattan and bid higher than local, debt-obligated newcomers. To an extent, Dollar General is the same but more sinister way of squeezing out grocers in food deserts in the name of continual profit growth. The key for the NYC restaurants is their ability to squeeze out local competition through being better guarantors or even potentially being a higher bidder of rent as large multinational corporations compared to local food. This has led to the corporatization of both Williamsburg and, of course, Manhattan. This is also why socialism is so attractive to people, because, truly, the extraction of rent by landlords is quite a problem but only because of municipal government mismanagement in developing the land to accommodate growth. Gentrification is natural and can kill certain cultures such as warehouse raves/art and nightlife, as covered by [About Here which also covers the nuance and tradeoff](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EmLO69c_fyw) of building more housing to stabilize market rental prices while killing certain culture.
- Supply chains like Sodexo or beef or basically the whole agriculture supply chain. Or even NYC’s monopolistic distributors which has led to bodegaffication instead of having more grocers and more cheap grocers leading to little diversity and again extracting rent from everyone along the market from farmer to consumer, etc.

These are distortions in the market in that they utilize their cash not to compete fairly for the hearts and minds of consumers but rather to shut out consumers’ wishes and against their will.

To me, the bottom line is business growth is not necessarily bad. Moving upmarket is and should be desired by businesses when opportunities arise and the necessity by the customers grows. The distortionary effects are more specific: the deployment of capital at scale, whether the environment is incentivized to make entire audience segments ignored or used to simply upmarket then enshittify or the capital is used to destroy competition when consumers were unwilling for the old businesses to die, distorts the market only if used to destroy the market intentionally to the firm’s benefit instead of the customer. It’s possible in the short term those bought out handyman services from PE start off ok, but they end up enshittifying because the goal is huge profit margin. Profit is not inherently evil as many radical and/or uneducated progressives say. The problem is when profit is used to feed greed.

Even from a basic economics standpoint, we say profit maximization is the key, but the problem that many conservatives believed in from 1980-2010 and continue to falsely perceive themselves in 2010-present 2026 is that free market capitalism is good for the country. The country is meant for people; the deployment of capital must be managed because free market capitalism’s assumption was that competition would be healthy and self-regulated. As seen from anti-trust laws’ very existence, the free market is simply not self regulatable in the best interest of the customer. Healthy profits are required for sustainable businesses; monopolies such as Google deserve to be scrutinized for distortionary effects, but their technology is so good compared to anyone else that instead of stealing their profits straight up as tax revenue which doesn’t help the customer ie businesses buying ads, using price caps makes more sense. I think in healthily competitive industries like housing don’t necessarily need price caps as seen by NYC with the supply demand issue. People want to build, so let them build; on the other hand, the NYC pied a terre tax is good because housing is meant for people. Allowing rich people to live in one apartment is sufficient, but if they end up using up the limited land for barely any use, then that capital has distorted the market; even with new supply built, without blocking wealthy buyers, would the build out of supply ever be for the regular joe? Or would the market only be incentivized to build for the uber wealthy? And I think to hypothesize that scenario of free will build out without a pied a terre, in our economic age of growing wealth inequality, I do think the biggest hit would be Manhattan and nearby neighborhoods like LIC and Williamsburg where properties are bought but not used at great economic damage to the city as a whole. If the underlying land appreciates so fast that investment by wealthy land owners ends up not being for people, then that is hurting the end consumer.

To wrap up, I want to talk about money, profit, and greed. At the end of the day, I don’t think money is evil. It is an extremely efficient way, especially in the age of specialization and industrialized agriculture, of exchanging goods and services compared to our ancient bartering methods. Profit margin is a tool used to indicate the health of a business with their cash flow, the efficiency of their services (which is the metric that the Tokyo, French, and NYC metro use), and, from the business’s side, an indicator for how well their business is doing and a metric to measure how well they can sustain growth in case of catastrophic events or necessity to innovate for continued competition. Innovation comes in the form of technological advancement but also in the form of marketing. Sure, profit seems directly tied to sales and marketing where you’re attracting customers, but I also find innovation to be tied to those two processes as well. They are repeatable things to garner the right audience to come chat, but their usage is also a form of indicating how saturated they’ve made their product in the market. Has a product or service reached its entire audience yet? Profit (not profit margin) is a good indicator of that.

However, it is the mission of greed where people choose to use money to sack competition without the consumers benefiting of such actions that I despise. Sometimes, it’s not the CEO that is evil but the system that makes the rest of the industry insufferable. This is the case of software where the venture capital environment makes it difficult for normal midsize bootstrapped businesses to thrive. Enshittification arises from the constant stomping out of competition either through being outgunned by cash or manpower. Of course, this merely only applies to larger markets. A Google Doc extension is naturally going to be bootstrapped due to hypothesized low business market cap.

On the other hand, to lend credit to the constant outcry of greed is evil and individually greedy people exist and do harm to the world, I agree they do exist, and a system that’s evil does not mean the person themself wasn’t evil to begin with. This is namely PE: as a finance friend said, a lot of these PE people are just finding ways to make money at any cost. They’re willing to scheme to steal from their LPs, to sell their “friends” in PE complete dogshit, and pretend all they want for the sake of making money. Not all PE people are like that, which is why I say the system itself isn’t naturally evil; it’s just deployment n’t  of capital, and sometimes businesses actually turn around for the better of everyone because of PE. But the aforementioned handyman acquisition being the bigger public discussion of many industries going through that phase of boomers retiring and thus PE scooping up their businesses is to me largely not an issue of a successor (which is the excuse) but rather many people feeling pressure that PE’s large scale buyout of firms will squeeze out competition. I actually don’t think PE is naturally evil unlike venture capital for SaaS in large market verticals; rather, in this case, the deployment of capital is used to directly distort the market to the PE firm’s direct benefit and no one else.

That’s the conclusion. I do want to explore something personal: I previously wrote about my worry about OpenAI deploying their capital to destroy online businesses; their huge customer base and thus relationships, their talent base and talent prestige, and general giddy for investment meant they had the ability to pick and choose any industry to start a mini startup and destroy, à la Microsoft and Google. Yet, they didn’t, instead focusing on model improvements. Of which, I commend on the focus. However, is that possibility real? I genuinely believe so as the aforementioned two behemoths have shown their ability to enter into random B2B SaaS markets. Though they make up an extremely small percent of revenue (last I checked their 10Ks, they always made up <15% of revenue outside their primary, original source of revenue), OpenAI with their frontier models theoretically could deploy swarms of teams and AI agents to eliminate the competition through a mix of cheap engineering, tons of capital, and tons of existing relationships. I don’t even think the products have to even be related to each other; for Google, I don’t really see how their products tie that well between each other (they do, but just barely), even within the same division like the various Google Cloud products or Google Suite.

But upon reflection, it does seem like being generally focused on a single business at a time is more likely to succeed. I wanted to build a LinkedIn competitor and learn how to best manage, hire, and compete in every industry, and deploy my own firms in any industry of interest on repeat (because the entire recruiting industry’s market cap including LinkedIn and agencies is 800B on my estimation, though that excludes BestFit’s management consulting side plus potential for creating disparate subsidiary companies). In the F500, most firms aren’t even software, and a lot are fashion, so I thought why not learn more about fashion (I have moral and now ethical/encironmental objections to fast fashion, so I’m not sure if I ever would’ve entered the space based on economics of being environmentally more friendly unless we could insutrialize clothing green-ness). Upon seeing those 10K numbers, I’m proud of America; there is so much capital investments flowing that founders all the time would much rather raise than be an executive to pitch an idea if the idea is big and good enough. That within large companies, the executive decision making bureacry’s ability to actually allow a subsidiary to be created is so slim that AWS and Amazon Prime was almost not created. This is precisely why Google Ventures and Microsoft Ventures exists, and why big companies constantly buy out smaller companies. An independently created firm historically speaking seems to do better than one spun out because of focus on particular audience and being completely unconstrained through independent of anyone else like executives or reputation from a brand.
