description: "Why it's harder to stop behavior than to encourage it"
pubDate: 2026-11-03

A discussion on why when something is pushed to the public, it gains supporters and detractors, and why once there are supporters, in a polarized world of propaganda and a lawyerly society where the basic unit of democracy lacks nuance in thinking and a land full of aggressive lawsuits, it hurts marginal improvement.

Concisely, why is it harder to stop behavior than to encourage?

Examples:

- One child policy ending later than needed. Starvation had been solved in China, yet there were too many people employed, so it'd be uneconomical directly to lay off the hundreds of thousands of workers enforcing the policy
- Gay marriage rights and gay rights in general have been opposed for centuries around the world, primarily by the population's moral and religious makeup. Once gay rights were legalized, America's precedent with gay rights were free. No matter religious zealots' opposition, gay rights are probably now permanent, especially as they are becoming more engrained in the Republican Party, the primary party purportedly against it or at least statistically based on its voter base.
- Cancel culture happened during the era of wokeism — when folks were trying to impose virtue signaling and moral purity, both as a personal display and against others who might simply have a different view. Woke is controversial in itself because it forces social behavior modification, but suddenly people who had no problems with an individual might hear that a figure was cancelled by mass protest online for something minor like a comment or an action. You suddenly had mob protests against an individual, and many fans were left defending against mobs because they saw figures, idols, and their work as part of their identity, like in k-pop or with streamers. The fans always lost though, or had to concede, even if some commentary was just one's opinion. Cancel culture happened not only to public figures; people in the workplace became scared of speaking too. If you didn't support abortion, you could be cancelled online, particularly in leftist digital spaces, even though that view is still held by a large minority in America. What I want to bring up is not just that wokeism was encouraged rather than discouraged until it became completely intolerable, but that whoever had been cancelled will always find some kind of supporter and some detractor. Before making my blog public, I feared backlash for my opinions. Then I became comfortable not only being wrong but having tons of people be angry at me for having an opinion that differed from theirs, particularly on Reddit (finding other intelligent people who don't argue based on emotion is always a bright spot).
