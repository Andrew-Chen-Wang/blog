I just watched a bunch of Asian kids like Yiheng Wang do cubing at 3-4 seconds average pace. To this day, I'm not sure
how people thought Chess players would be good programmers. The rationale was that Chess was logical, and programming
is naturally logical. But I've never found Chess to be that logical? I think Rubik's cube solvers is a better analogy.
There are tons of algorithms and strategies to solving Rubik's cubes, but now that we have competitive cubing averaging
3-5 seconds, the strategy is even more interesting than before. Before, most people were simply aiming to solve a cube
as fast as possible with the best memorized algorithm based on single turns. I still remember a lot of tips about
learning as many algorithms as possible and choosing the one with the least turns.

But the complication has arisen in that your hands can only move so fast, so cubers do parallel turns. This means the
most algorithm isn't necessarily the least number of turns or most optimal turns, but rather the most optimal turns
that maximizes using all your fingers in parallel like spinning two different sides that are unblocked from each other.
I think that's a great way of analogizing parallel computing and deciding how to parallelize processes to be faster
than simply maxing out CPU/RAM and continuously optimizing code assuming you're on a single process.

Side note, after these children solve their Rubik's cubes, they start messing, decompressing, destressing might I say
by ferociously turning a second cube on the table? It might be for competition reasons like changing up a cube, but
I think it's like their form of a smoke break to let out stress lmao.
